You pay the toll. A private consortium keeps it until 2061.

The express lanes on I-35W, I-820, SH 121/183 and LBJ sit on state highways. The tolls go to investor groups led by Ferrovial, a company now based in Amsterdam, under contracts that run for roughly fifty years. Here's who they are, what the state put in, and what we're asking the Legislature to do.

What you're actually paying for

Texas owns the land and the highway.11 But on these corridors, the state signed comprehensive development agreements: a private group rebuilt the road, added toll lanes, and in exchange earned the right to set prices and collect tolls on those lanes for decades.1

The prices aren't fixed. They're recalculated every few minutes to keep the toll lanes moving at around 50 mph, and the contracts allow them to go above the normal cap when traffic is heavy.1213 That's how a short trip ends up costing more than $20.

Who's collecting

Each road is run by its own company, owned by a small group of investors. The lead investor on all three is Cintra, the toll road arm of Ferrovial.

This isn't about where the investors are from. These are mainstream investors from allied countries, and a Texas pension fund holds a stake in LBJ. The problem is the arrangement: a public highway, private control over pricing, a fifty-year term, and very little public reporting on what drivers pay or where it goes.

Ask the 90th Legislature to act

Lawmakers return to Austin in January 2027. We're asking them to cap surge tolls, make these companies open their books, and price out what it would cost to end the contracts early.
