How a public road gets a private price

Texas owns the land and the highway.11 But in 2009 the state signed long contracts with private groups: they rebuilt the road, added toll lanes, and in exchange got the right to set prices and keep the tolls on those lanes for about fifty years.1

The prices aren't fixed. They're recalculated every five minutes to keep the toll lanes moving at 50 mph, and the contracts let them go past the normal cap when traffic is heavy.1213 That's how one trip ends up costing more than $20.

Who's collecting

Each road is run by its own company, owned by a small group of investors. The lead investor on all three is Cintra, the toll road arm of Ferrovial, now headquartered in Amsterdam.18

This isn't about where the investors are from. They're mainstream investors from allied countries, and a Texas pension fund holds a stake in LBJ. The problem is the deal: a public highway, private control of the price, a fifty-year term, and almost no public reporting on what drivers pay or where it goes.
